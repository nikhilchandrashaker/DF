A hypothetical rule, tested against 150 seasons

What if fielding didn't require hitting?

Baseball let pitchers stop batting in 1973. It never asked the mirror question: could a great glove stop batting too? This is a counterfactual test of a “designated fielder,” built from the Lahman database, 1876–2025.

Two different questions

Individual suitability
Which player-seasons look like a DF — great glove, weak bat, relative to their league and position?

Team impact
Did a specific team actually have the roster pieces — a bench bat, a better glove — to gain runs from the swap?

Kept deliberately separate. One is an input to the other — not the answer.

Defense doesn't convert to runs cleanly — and pretending it does breaks the model

A first pass converted fielding-range differences into runs at ~0.75 runs/play, the textbook constant. It produced single-season values of +150 to +200 runs — nonsense, since even the best real defensive seasons top out near +30.

Why: putouts mix real skill with a team's groundball rate, park, and pitching staff — context only batted-ball data can separate. So defense here is a position-season z-score, not invented runs.

The team-level swap, in three roles

Incumbent — today's real starter: bats and fields the position.

DF candidate — the teammate with the best glove at that spot, only if better than the incumbent's.

Displaced hitter — the best bench bat, who inherits the incumbent's plate appearances.

Net Offensive Swap = (displaced hitter's runs/PA − incumbent's runs/PA) × incumbent's actual PA — real runs, no defensive assumption required.

What this doesn't prove

Some “displaced hitters” (Mark McGwire, 2000 Cardinals) had few plate appearances from a mid-season trade — not from being buried for a bad glove. The model can't yet tell those apart.

Still: two-thirds of team-seasons in baseball history had a real bench bat sitting behind a fielder who didn't need to hit as well as he did — and the rule that would have unlocked it never existed.
